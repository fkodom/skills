## Quick Summary

- Four players form two teams: the Allies and the Axis.
- Each team receives a secret mission with eight objectives.
- Each player knows four objectives their teammate does not.
- After a short planning period, teammates may communicate only through coded messages in one shared email thread.
- Each round, both teams may send one coded message.
- During codebreaking periods, each team may publicly declare one enemy objective it believes it has discovered.
- A correct declaration invalidates that objective and forces the enemy to communicate a replacement.
- To finish, both teammates publicly submit the four objectives originally assigned to their partner.
- The game lasts no more than 25 rounds.

## 1. Setup

A neutral moderator administers the game. The moderator may be a person or an AI agent.

Each team receives a mission containing:

1. Operation codename
2. Agent codename
3. Rendezvous location
4. Target
5. Date and time
6. Package, equipment, or intelligence
7. Escape or extraction route
8. Authentication phrase

The moderator assigns four objectives to each player by direct email.

Each player initially knows only their own four objectives. Objective ownership remains fixed throughout the game, even if an objective is later replaced.

## 2. Pregame Planning

Before receiving their objectives, teammates may communicate privately and agree on:

- An opening cipher and key
- Ways to change keys or ciphers
- Formatting or authentication conventions
- Who should send during different rounds
- A general communication strategy

Teams may not prepare a complete codebook of possible mission answers or a long list of unrelated keys for future rounds.

After the objectives are distributed, private communication between teammates ends.

## 3. Schedule

The game lasts no more than 25 rounds.

Both teams act simultaneously.

Each round lasts 48 hours and is divided into two periods. Every period begins and ends at 12:00 p.m. Central Time, observing CST or CDT as applicable.

### Transmission Period

From noon until noon the following day, each team may:

- Post one coded message, or
- Announce its final submission

### Codebreaking Period

During the following noon-to-noon period:

- Players work on decoding the public messages.
- Each active team may post one interception declaration.

The moderator resolves the round at noon and opens the next one.

## 4. The Shared Email Thread

All coded messages, interception declarations, moderator updates, and final submissions are posted in one shared email thread containing all four players and the moderator.

Everyone may examine every message from every round.

The moderator may use direct email only to:

- Send initial objectives
- Send replacement objectives
- Answer administrative questions

Players may not use direct email to communicate with teammates.

## 5. Sending Coded Messages

Each team may post one coded message during each transmission period.

Only one teammate may send it. If both teammates send, only the first message counts.

A coded message may contain no more than 1,000 characters, including spaces and punctuation.

Each message may communicate only:

> One genuine mission objective.

The message may also contain:

- Cover text
- Padding or nulls
- Greetings and signatures
- Hidden key indicators
- Misleading non-mission information
- Instructions for future cipher or key changes
- Unusual spacing or formatting

Messages may resemble letters, reports, orders, weather updates, shopping lists, or other ordinary communications.

Players do not report friendly decoding results to the moderator. Each player must keep their own record of what they believe their teammate communicated.

See Appendix A for permitted ciphers.

## 6. Interception Declarations

During each codebreaking period, each active team may make one public interception declaration.

Either teammate may post it. If both post declarations, only the first counts.

A declaration must name:

1. One enemy objective category
2. Its exact current value

Example:

> Allied interception declaration:
> Axis rendezvous location: the abandoned customs house outside Calais.

The declaration may be based on:

- The current round's message
- Any previous message
- A pattern across several messages
- A discovered key-change system
- Any other information in the shared thread

A team may secretly break a code and wait until a later round before declaring what it knows.

No explanation or cipher key is required. An exact lucky guess also counts.

## 7. Resolving Interceptions

### Successful Declaration

A declaration succeeds if its category and value exactly match a current enemy objective.

The moderator then:

1. Announces that the declaration succeeded.
2. Invalidates the compromised value.
3. Creates a replacement.
4. Sends the replacement directly to the player who originally owned that objective.

The moderator does not publicly reveal the replacement.

The objective's owner must communicate the new value to their teammate through a later coded message.

### Failed Declaration

If either the category or value is wrong, the declaration fails.

The attempt is still used for that round, but the team may try again later.

A value that was once correct no longer counts after it has been replaced.

## 8. Final Submission

A team may announce its final submission instead of sending a coded message.

The announcement must be public:

> The Allies are making their final submission.

The announcement is irreversible and consumes the team's transmission action.

Once the announcement is posted:

- That team's objectives are frozen.
- No further interception declarations may target that team.
- The team sends no more coded messages.
- The team makes no more interception declarations.

During the following noon-to-noon codebreaking period, both teammates must publicly submit their answers.

### What Each Player Submits

Each player submits only the four objectives that were originally assigned to their teammate.

For example:

- Player One originally received objectives 1, 3, 5, and 7.
- Player Two originally received objectives 2, 4, 6, and 8.
- Player One submits objectives 2, 4, 6, and 8.
- Player Two submits objectives 1, 3, 5, and 7.

This proves that mission information was communicated in both directions.

If an objective was replaced during the game, the player must submit its current replacement value.

Because the teammates submit different categories, neither can copy answers from the other's public submission.

## 9. Submission Scoring

Each objective is worth one point.

A team receives:

- 1 point for each exact current objective value submitted correctly
- 0 points for an incorrect or missing answer

The maximum score is 8 points.

The team's official submission time is the timestamp of the second teammate's completed submission.

A teammate who misses the deadline scores zero for all four of their assigned answers.

Once a team announces its final submission, it is finished playing, regardless of its score.

## 10. Winning

### Perfect Submission

A team that scores 8 out of 8 wins as soon as the moderator verifies both submissions.

### Imperfect First Submission

If the first team to submit scores fewer than eight points:

- Its score becomes the score to beat.
- That team is finished playing.
- The opposing team continues without further enemy messages or interceptions.
- The opposing team wins only by scoring higher.

If the second team ties or scores lower, the first team wins.

### Simultaneous Submissions

If both teams announce during the same transmission period:

1. The higher score wins.
2. If the scores are equal, the earlier official submission time wins.

## 11. Round 25

Round 25 is the final round.

Any team still active during Round 25 must make its final submission instead of sending another coded message.

If both teams make their first submissions in Round 25:

1. The higher score wins.
2. If the scores are equal, the earlier official submission time wins.

If one team submitted earlier, the remaining team must exceed that earlier score.

## 12. Moderator Updates

At the end of each normal round, the moderator posts a brief update.

Example:

> Round 6 complete.
> One Axis interception succeeded.
> Round 7 begins now.

The moderator should announce:

- Whether each interception succeeded or failed
- Which teams remain active
- When the next period begins

The moderator should not reveal:

- Undeclared objective values
- Replacement values
- Whether teammates correctly decoded friendly messages
- How close a team is to finishing

## 13. Strategy

Using the same cipher throughout the game is simple but risky. Once the enemy understands it, they may revisit every previous message.

More advanced teams may develop ways to change:

- Keys
- Substitution alphabets
- Reading routes
- Cipher types

Possible signals include:

- Sign-offs
- Round numbers
- Message lengths
- Previous plaintext
- Hidden instructions in cover text

The best system must be understandable to your teammate, difficult for the enemy to discover, and recoverable when something goes wrong.

Be creative, take risks, and have fun.

## Appendix A: Permitted Ciphers

### General Rule

Each message may use one cipher that can reasonably be encoded and decoded with pen and paper.

Permitted ciphers fall into two broad families:

- Fixed alphabetic substitution
- Transposition

Players may invent variations within these families.

Adding cover text, spacing, punctuation, or padding does not count as applying another cipher.

Encrypting a message and then encrypting the result again is not allowed.

### Fixed Alphabetic Substitution

Each plaintext letter is consistently replaced by another letter.

#### Caesar Shift

Move every letter the same number of places through the alphabet.

With a shift of three:

> ATTACK → DWWDFN

#### Atbash

Reverse the alphabet:

> A becomes Z, B becomes Y, and so on.

#### Keyword Alphabet

Write the unrepeated letters of a keyword, followed by the unused letters of the alphabet.

Using `FALCON`:

> Plain: ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ
> Cipher: FALCONBDEGHIJKMPQRSTUVWXYZ

#### Arbitrary Substitution

Use any scrambled alphabet, provided every plaintext letter always has the same replacement.

Other fixed mathematical or patterned substitutions are also allowed if they can be worked by hand.

### Transposition

Transposition ciphers keep the original letters but rearrange their order.

#### Columnar Transposition

Write the message in rows beneath numbered columns or a keyword, then read the columns in a chosen order.

#### Route Transposition

Write the message in a grid and read it along an agreed route.

Examples include:

- Across rows
- Down columns
- Alternating upward and downward
- Diagonally
- Around the outside
- Spiraling inward or outward
- Beginning from a chosen corner

Other manually reproducible arrangements are allowed.

### Padding and Cover Text

Players may add letters, words, or sentences that are not part of the genuine objective.

Padding may be used to:

- Hide the message length
- Disguise repeated words
- Complicate frequency analysis
- Conceal where the ciphertext begins
- Carry key-management information

The intended teammate must have a reasonable way to identify or remove it.

Padding may not communicate another mission objective.

### Prohibited Methods

The following are not allowed:

- Vigenère or other polyalphabetic ciphers
- Multiple ciphers applied in succession
- One-time pads
- Modern digital encryption
- Codebooks containing every possible mission answer
- Long lists of unrelated prearranged keys
- Brute-force computer searches
- Automated cipher solvers
- AI-assisted codebreaking
- Software that identifies or decrypts ciphers

Players may use:

- Paper and pencil
- Reference sheets
- Frequency tables
- Personal notes
- Ordinary calculators
- Instructions explaining permitted ciphers
